Query-Conditioned Articulation Estimation from a Single Image
Abdelrhman Werby, Fabio Scaparro, Kai O. Arras
Abstract— Enabling robots to estimate the kinematic param-
eters of articulated objects unlocks a wide range of capabilities
for interaction and manipulation. The estimation has to happen
from the information the robot currently observes, often just a
single RGB image of an object it has never seen before. Current
single-image approaches couple articulation part segmentation
with articulation estimation, making their predictions vulnerable
to missed detections and incorrect part associations, and they
regress metric 3D geometry that a single view fixes only up to
scale. We present QueryArt, a model that estimates articulation
parameters from a single RGB image, a 2D query point, and
camera intrinsics. QueryArt is trained to estimate the 3D
articulation geometry relative to the queried point and in
units of its depth, which keeps its target identifiable from
the image alone. A single depth measurement at the query point
then supplies the scale and recovers the metric parameters.
We train QueryArt on a curated mixture of synthetic and
real-world articulation datasets. We evaluate QueryArt on
several benchmarks and compare it against recent baselines.
QueryArt outperforms recent baselines on most articulation
metrics, including on out-of-distribution data. To demonstrate
the model’s capabilities in real-world settings, we evaluate
QueryArt on a mobile manipulator across 57 manipulation
trials spanning 16 object parts and five viewpoint classes,
achieving a 70.2% success rate. We provide code and videos at:
https://abwerby.github.io/queryart/.
I. INTRODUCTION
Robots deployed in human-centered environments must
interact with articulated objects such as doors, cabinets, draw-
ers, and appliances [1], [2]. Successfully manipulating such
objects requires estimating their underlying kinematic model,
including the joint type, motion axis, and axis location. These
parameters support a wide range of downstream capabilities,
from navigating through closed doors to searching for objects
inside cabinets and drawers. Ideally, a robot should infer them
for an unseen object before physical interaction, using only
the observations available from its current viewpoint.
Single-view articulation estimation methods [3]–[6] jointly
detect and segment movable parts and estimate their motion
parameters from a single image. This formulation couples
articulation estimation with part localization; as a result,
a localization failure propagates directly to the kinematic
prediction. Recent approaches [7], [8] instead predict a 3D
interaction trajectory from a single image using a learned
motion prior, but their output is not parameterized by an
explicit revolute or prismatic joint model and therefore does
not guarantee kinematic consistency by construction. Both
families of methods regress metric 3D geometry directly

To this end, we present QueryArt, a model for estimating
articulation parameters from a single calibrated RGB image
and a 2D query point on the articulated part (Fig. 1). It predicts
the joint type, the 3D motion axis, and the query-relative
axis location, normalized by the query point’s depth. This
normalization makes the target invariant to uniform scaling of
the scene. At inference time, obtaining the query point’s depth
provides the metric scale, removing the need for the model
to infer absolute size from appearance. Conditioning on an
interaction point decouples the question of where to interact
from the question of how the selected point moves, allowing
the query to originate from a human click or an independent
affordance-grounding system [12], [13]. The model com-
bines a frozen visual foundation encoder [14], deformable

attention [15], and lightweight articulation-specific heads.
QueryArt is trained on a curated mixture of two synthetic
and three real-world articulation datasets. When the query
point metric depth is available, a deterministic lifting step
recovers the metric 3D motion axis. Across held-out splits
of four training datasets and two datasets excluded from
training entirely, QueryArt improves over recent single-image
baselines on most articulation metrics, and 57 trials on a
mobile manipulator show that its predictions are accurate
enough to open furniture the model has never seen, at a
70.2% success rate.
Our main contributions are:
• A query-conditioned formulation that models articula-
tion estimation as regression of normalized articulation
geometry, given a single calibrated RGB image and a
2D query point.
• An efficient architecture that combines high-resolution
frozen visual foundation features and lightweight predic-
tion heads with a compact, physically structured axis-
hinge representation.
• An extensive evaluation on synthetic and real-world
articulation benchmarks, including out-of-distribution
data, demonstrating improved articulation estimation
over recent baselines.
• Real-robot experiments showing that the predicted
parameters support analytic motion generation and
manipulation of previously unseen objects.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Single RGB-based Articulation Estimation
Single-image articulation estimation, such as OPD and
OPDMulti [3], [4], jointly detects openable parts and regresses
their joint type, motion axis, and origin, coupling articulation
estimation with part localization. 3DOI [16] predicts inter-
action properties at a queried image location, including a
projected 2D rotation axis, but does not recover a complete
3D articulation model. CARTO [17] reconstructs the shape,
pose, and joint state of multiple articulated objects from a
single stereo RGB observation, but represents each object as a
whole rather than conditioning on a queried interaction point.
A3VLM [6] uses vision-language models to predict joint
type and 3D motion axes from object-part boxes or textual
descriptions, while other methods [7], [8] directly predict
3D interaction trajectories from a single image using learned
motion priors. Such methods do not explicitly parameterize
motion as revolute or prismatic and therefore do not enforce
the corresponding kinematic structure by construction.
B. Articulation Estimation from 3D Observations
Several approaches estimate articulation directly from
3D geometry. ANCSH [18] estimates category-level part
poses and joint parameters from a single depth image using
a normalized coordinate hierarchy, while ScrewNet [19]
predicts category-independent articulation models from depth
observations using a screw-theoretic parameterization, and
Zeng et al. [20] identify the articulation mechanism between
part pairs from a single RGB-D frame. FlowBot++ [1] is the
closest to our geometric parameterization, as it regresses the
perpendicular displacement from every point of an object to
the joint axis, which is the same quantity our model predicts
at the query point. However, it operates on a point cloud
and relies on a part segmentation mask, and it regresses the
displacement in metric scale, which a single image cannot
determine. DUST-Net [21] predicts distributions over articula-
tion parameters from depth observations, explicitly modeling
uncertainty over joint type, axis, and configuration. More
recent scene-level methods [9] operate on reconstructed 3D
point clouds to jointly segment articulated parts and estimate
their motion parameters. Other approaches jointly recover
object geometry and articulation from observations of multiple
object states. Ditto [22] reconstructs articulated objects and
estimates their kinematic models from observations before
and after interaction, and Weng et al. [23] build digital
twins of unknown articulated objects from two RGB-D scans.
PARIS [24], ArtGS [25], and ScrewSplat [26] respectively
optimize a part-level radiance, Gaussian, or screw-based
representation jointly with the articulation parameters from
multi-view observations of different joint states. While these
methods benefit from explicit 3D geometry, they require
depth, reconstructed scenes, or observations of multiple
configurations, limiting their applicability when a robot
encounters an unseen object from a single view.
C. Multiple-Observation Articulation Estimation
A complementary line of work infers articulation by
observing how an object moves. Sturm et al. [27] fit
and select rigid, revolute, and prismatic kinematic models
from sequences of observed relative part poses, performing
system identification after motion has been observed and
thereby resolving ambiguities that appearance alone leaves
open. Interactive-perception systems obtain this motion by
manipulating the object themselves [28]–[30], which Ditto
in the House [31] scales to room-scale scenes, while factor-
graph formulations [32], [33] track articulated parts online and
fuse visual with proprioceptive measurements. ArtiPoint [10]
instead tracks parts through egocentric RGB-D sequences of
human interaction. Although these methods provide accurate
kinematic estimates from observed motion, they require
multiple observations or an interaction trajectory before
the articulation can be inferred. In contrast, we predict
plausible articulation hypotheses before interaction from a
single monocular observation.

B. Model Architecture
QueryArt’s architecture is illustrated in Fig. 2. Given a
single RGB image I, a pixel query q = (u, v), and camera
intrinsics K, we proceed as follows:
We start by extracting image features using the frozen
DINOv3 ViT-B/16 image encoder [14], producing 768-
channel patch features with a stride of 16. Following [34],
we keep early features in block 4 and late features in the final
block of the transformer model. Then we add the two features,
weighted by a learned sigmoid scalar, and project them to
256 channels. Estimating articulation parameters requires
the model to locate fine-grained details and understand the
global semantic context; therefore, we construct a three-scale
feature pyramid [35] using three sequential 3×3 convolutional
blocks, downsampling the fused features Fimg ∈RS×S×256
to resolutions S, S/2, and S/4, respectively.
We create a query embedding that combines three com-
plementary signals. First, we normalize the pixel query
to ¯q ∈[0, 1]2, then encode it using a Fourier positional
encoding [36]. Second, we bilinearly sample the final DINOv3
block feature at the query location, giving a 768-channel
descriptor. Third, we construct the calibration vector

E. Implementation
The DINOv3 encoder is frozen; only the query encoder,
decoder, and output heads are trained, for a total of 4.7M
learnable parameters. We initialize these from scratch and
train with AdamW on one NVIDIA RTX5090 GPU, with
a learning rate of 10−4, weight decay of 5 × 10−4, and a
cosine schedule decaying to 10−6 over at most 15 full epochs.
The optimizer batch size is 64, dropout is 0.1, and gradients
are clipped to norm 5. Learned computations use bfloat16
mixed precision, while geometric normalization, projection,
and losses use float32. We train for at least four full epochs,
then run early stopping with a patience of three.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Data Generation
We train our model on five sources: three real-world
datasets, Articulate3D [9], SceneFun3D [38], and Multi-
Scan [37], and two synthetic ones, PartNet-Mobility [39]
and HSSD [40]. The real-world datasets provide articulation
annotations and posed RGB frames, with depth obtained from
sensors or reconstructed geometry; for the synthetic datasets,
we render RGB-D frames and extract joint parameters from
the simulator. We sample frames containing visible articulated
parts and apply source-specific viewpoint-diversity checks.
We convert source articulation annotations or simulator joint
parameters into the camera coordinates of each sampled
frame. Query points are sampled from visible part masks,
with adaptive margins from mask boundaries, and retained
only when their depth is valid and lies between 0.05 and
3 m. For the real-world training datasets, we retain the source
train, validation, and test splits.
Across all splits, Articulate3D contributes 2.6K views and
68K query points covering 1.3K distinct moving parts across
182 scenes. SceneFun3D contributes 3.4K views and 74K
query points covering 1K distinct moving parts across 176
scene visits. MultiScan contributes 5K views and 146K query
points covering 2.2K distinct moving parts across 227 scans
of 96 environments. Our PartNet-Mobility renders contribute
30K views and 578K query points from 843 distinct object
assets. Our HSSD renders contribute 22K views and 300K
query points from 3K object instances, representing 1.5K
distinct assets across 161 scenes. HSSD is used for training
only; the held-out evaluations in Tab. I cover the remaining
four sources.
To assess our model’s generalization capabilities, we
construct out-of-distribution evaluation sets from two real-
world datasets, Arti4D [10] and HOI! [41]. We convert their
annotations into the same representation and apply the same
depth-validity checks, with additional manual annotation.
These datasets provide additional real-world environments
and viewpoints, and are used only for evaluation; they are
excluded from our training data.

Baselines. We compare against two types of baselines.
The first set, OPDFormer-C [4], MOPD [5], 3DOI [16],
and A3VLM [6], predicts articulation parameters and part
segments from a single RGB image. The second set, Mol-
moMotion [8] and VidBot [7], predicts a point trajectory in

that segment multiple parts within the image, we select the
predicted articulation parameters whose mask overlaps the
GT queries on the target object. We provide VidBot with
GT 3D start and end points, and MolmoMotion with up to
eight 3D query points and a language prompt. For both, we
report only the RMSE metric. For all baselines, we use their
publicly released pretrained checkpoints.

D. Real-Robot Experiments
To test whether our model’s predictions can drive real-
world manipulation of articulated objects and generalize to
unseen object instances, we design and conduct a real-world
experiment on a mobile manipulation platform.
Hardware Setup. We use a Boston Dynamics Spot
equipped with an arm, and capture RGB images directly
from its in-hand camera at a resolution of 640 × 480px.
QueryArt is deployed on an NVIDIA Jetson Thor payload
which runs on the back of the robot.
Experiment Setup. We run the experiment on common
articulated furniture, such as drawers, cabinets, and doors,
none of which the model saw during training. The experiments
consist of 57 trials, spanning 16 articulated object parts, with
viewpoints grouped into five classes: close, mid, far, left,
and right. For each object and view, we manually supply
a candidate grasping point at which we query the model.
This is typically the handle of the object, so that the same
location serves both as the query pixel for the model and as
the robot’s grasp target. Combined with the metric depth from
the in-hand depth camera at the query pixel, the prediction
yields the metric-scale 3D joint parameters. The robot then
grasps the handle using its own proprietary API, and executes
the trajectory to interact with the part. The opening trajectory
is directly extracted from QueryArt’s prediction and followed
using a Cartesian controller for the end-effector. A trial is
counted as a success if the robot opens the part to the target
range of motion: 20 cm for prismatic joints and 45◦for
revolute joints, and as a failure otherwise. All trials that
were unsuccessful due to grasping failure were not scored
and not counted in the final results. The remaining failure
cases are directly attributable to wrong predictions.
Results. Across the 57 trials, the robot succeeded in 40
and failed in 17, a 70.2% success rate. Figure 4 breaks the
trials down by viewpoint class. We observe that the model’s
accuracy degrades when the viewpoint is at an extreme angle
or when the camera cannot fully observe the object.

directly regressed metric offset retains whatever scale was
learned during training. Consistently with this, the degradation
concentrates on the hinge error, while the scale-free axis
direction is not consistently affected. Finally, replacing the
two direction heads with a single head shared by both joint
types also degrades most metrics, indicating that revolute and
prismatic directions are better estimated separately.

V. CONCLUSION
In this work, we presented QueryArt, a model for estimating
articulation parameters from a single calibrated RGB image
and a 2D query point. The model predicts the joint type,
the 3D axis direction, and, for revolute joints, the 3D
offset of the axis relative to the query point, expressed in
units of the query point’s depth. Unlike prior single-image
methods, which first segment the moving part and regress
metric 3D parameters directly, this formulation decouples
where to interact from how the selected point moves; the
depth-normalized representation removes the dependence on
an absolute scale that a single view cannot resolve. This
formulation aligns well with robotics settings where the
robot must interact with unseen objects based on a single
observation. Across four held-out splits of in-distribution data
and two out-of-distribution datasets, QueryArt demonstrates
robust performance and transfers to unseen object instances
and viewpoints without retraining or fine-tuning. Real-robot
trials further show that the predicted parameters can be
scaled to metric units from a single depth reading, applied


to previously unseen furniture, and used to successfully
manipulate it.
