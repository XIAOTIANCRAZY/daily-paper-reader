# Screw Attention: Rigid-Body Algebra Inside a Transformer 

Aly Magassouba 

Abstract —Learned manipulation policies rediscover from data the spatial relations that rigid-body mechanics supplies in closed form. This costs data, and it leaves the policies fragile to geometric changes in the scene. We present Screw Attention, a transformer layer in which the relation between two bodies is a spatial transform rather than a graph edge. Every token is a body with a pose. Each pair of tokens carries the relative pose and, for robot joints, the joint screw. Messages are transported along this relation into the receiver’s frame, while the attention scores see only frame-invariant quantities. By construction, the messages are equivariant to an independent change of frame at every token, and a single layer can express the velocity recursion of rigid-body mechanics. On simulated manipulation tasks, Screw Attention matches or outperforms controls of the same size, including graph, transformer and flat networks on LIBERO-Spatial. With 16,162 parameters it reaches 97.3% on LIBERO-Spatial from object poses (without images or language), above a flat network with 27 × more parameters. Under a change of per-link frame convention its success is unchanged, while every other learned network falls below 3%. Placed on an analytic controller as a gated residual, it raises insertion success by 17.3 points. It is unaffected by pose noise up to 10 mm and by joint offsets within the factory calibration of a Franka arm. These results suggest a criterion: geometry is decisive when the task requires relations between frames that no other part of the system supplies. Code and trained policies will be released. 

Index Terms —robot learning, transformers, equivariance, spa-tial algebra, screw theory, manipulation 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# LEARNED policies have become one of the main ap-proaches to robot manipulation, using scale as their principal lever. For instance, vision-language-action models with billions of parameters, trained on large corpora of demon-strations, now perform multi-step tasks from raw images and natural language. However, recent robustness analyses expose a sharp weakness: displacing the manipulated objects, or altering the robot’s initial configuration, degrades the accuracy of such policies drastically [1], [2]. These failures are not only of perception, but of representing spatial relationships. Yet those relations are exactly what rigid-body mechanics supplies in closed form. The kinematic model specifies how motion at one joint propagates to distant links, how a force applied at the gripper is distributed across the actuators, and how the gripper pose relates to any commanded frame. These relations are available a priori, long before any data are collected. Classical controllers are built on this structure,

The limitations of this approach are equally well-defined: analytic methods cannot easily express contact, friction, deformation, uncertain payload properties, or any task whose objective is not a fixed set-point. In this respect, analytic methods and learned policies form two complementary paradigms. One is given the geometry but cannot represent what the model omits; the other can represent what the model omits but must rediscover the geometry. Several lines of work have therefore reintroduced struc-ture into learned policies, although each retains only part of it. Embodiment-aware policy transformers [3]–[5] encode the robot’s connectivity as an abstract graph, through per-limb descriptors or by biasing attention toward links that are neighbours in the kinematic tree. Equivariant manipulation policies [6]–[8] preserve the geometry of the scene, so that behaviour is consistent when the scene is transformed, but they represent the robot only through its end-effector pose. Kinematics-structured policies [9], [10] do carry the poses or transforms of individual links, yet process them with learned matrices, discarding the very structure that made those poses worth carrying. In all cases, the relation between two bodies is either abstracted into a graph or reduced to coordinates that a learned map is free to reinterpret. That relation is neither abstract nor arbitrary, and this observation underpins our approach. It is a physical relation arXiv:2610.00904v1 [cs.RO] 1 Oct 2026 SCREW ATTENTION 2

with explicit content: the distance between two bodies, their relative orientation, and, when a joint is present, the axis of rotation or translation. Robotics provides a concise formalism for exactly these notions, the spatial or screw algebra of rigid-body mechanics [11]–[13]. In this formalism such a relation is a single object and composing two of them determines directly how motion of one body induces motion of another. A policy given these relations is therefore not required to learn kinematics from scratch, since the kinematic structure is already encoded in the relations themselves. The question then becomes how to build an architecture that can make direct use of this kinematic structure, rather than re-learning it implicitly. To this end, we adopt a transformer [14] whose tokens exchange screws (the velocities and forces of rigid-body mechanics) rather than only feature vectors. As illustrated in Fig. 1, in our architecture, each input token is a body with a pose, which may be a robot link or an object in the scene. Standard transformers relate tokens only through a learned compatibility between their features. In our approach, each pair of tokens also carries a given term that represents the physical relation between the two bodies, read from the kinematic model or, for objects, from perception. This addition enables two architectural shifts. First, any message sent from one token to another is trans-formed into the receiving body’s own frame. Indeed, quantities defined at different locations cannot be combined until they are expressed in a common frame, so the message must be transformed before the receiving token can use it. Second, the attention weights access the relation only through frame-invariant quantities (e.g., distances, joint types, adjacency), which enter as an attention bias. The distinction matters: a bias on the scores is a reweighting a transformer can in principle learn unaided, whereas transporting a quantity between coordinate frames is an operation it cannot express on its own. The physics therefore enters where it adds genuine capability, on the messages. Three properties follow from this construction. (i) The recursion that propagates velocities along the kinematic chain lies within what the layer can express, so the policy need not learn mechanics from data and can spend its capacity on the phenomena that mechanics omits. (ii) The geometry is combined only through operations admitted by the spa-tial algebra, and these operations commute with changes of coordinate frame. As a result, the transported messages do not depend on how a frame is attached to a link, which is a modelling convention with no physical meaning. (iii) Because the readout emits the same command as an analytic controller, a joint torque or an end-effector twist, the layer can be used alone or on top of such a controller as a residual. Closing a gate recovers the controller exactly, so the balance between analytic and learned control is a choice made per task rather than fixed by the architecture. In robotics, every link carries its own frame. The choice of where that frame is attached is a convention that varies across description formats [15] and sometimes even across descriptions of the same robot [16]. A policy whose behaviour depends on this convention can fail on a robot that is phys-ically identical but described differently. Existing equivariant architectures [8], [17] are equivariant only to a single, global change of frame applied to the entire scene. By contrast, our layer is equivariant to an independent change of frame on every link, which is the freedom a robot description actually has. With this foundation, our contributions are as follows. 1) An embodiment-aware attention layer grounded in rigid-body mechanics. Tokens are related through the physical relation between bodies rather than an abstract graph, and messages are transported into the receiver’s coordinate frame. As a result, the computation of how motion propagates along the robot can be expressed by the layer rather than learned from data (Section III-D). Moreover, the layer can be used on its own or as a residual on top of an analytic controller. 2) An extensive empirical study across diverse tasks and a standard benchmark. We evaluate our ar-chitecture on three simulated manipulation tasks and on LIBERO-Spatial, comparing it against mechanism-matched controls. The structured policy outperforms graph, transformer and flat networks of the same size on LIBERO-Spatial, and even surpasses a flat network with 

27 × more parameters. Under changes to the per-link frame convention, it is the only model whose success is unchanged, whereas all controls collapse to 0.0–2.3%. Used on top of an analytic controller on a contact-rich insertion task, it raises the success rate by 17.3 points. These results show that the structure contributes capability (Sections IV, V and IV-G). 3) A hypothesis for when the structure is decisive .Across the tasks studied, we identify a simple condi-tion under which the geometry affects performance: the structure is decisive when the task requires reasoning over relations between coordinate frames that are not otherwise supplied by an analytic controller. When this condition is met, correct relations yield large gains over incorrect ones. When it is not, the correctness of the relations makes no measurable difference. This hypothesis is consistent with all results reported here (Section V, Appendix H). II. R ELATED WORK 

Four strands of prior work are directly relevant to this study: policy transformers that encode the robot’s structure, equivariant policies that preserve scene geometry, architectures that represent the robot’s kinematics explicitly, and methods that introduce physics through the training objective rather than the network. Each preserves a different aspect of the physical structure available to a manipulation policy. 

Embodiment-aware policy transformers. This strand aims to exploit the robot’s structure in a policy. MetaMorph [3] assigns a token to each limb and conditions it on per-limb mor-phology descriptors. Body Transformer [4] restricts attention using a mask derived from the body graph, and morphology-embedded transformers [5] add a topology-aware bias to the attention scores, with per-joint descriptors as ordinary features. In all cases, the kinematic tree enters only as connectivity: SCREW ATTENTION 3

which parts exist and which are connected. The physical content of the relation between two links, how far apart they are and about which axis one moves relative to the other, is however left for the network to infer. The scene is likewise represented as flat features rather than as additional bodies with poses. 

Equivariant manipulation policies. Whereas the first strand structures the robot, this strand structures the scene. Mo-tivated by data efficiency and by generalisation to new object placements, Diffusion-EDFs [6], RiEMann [18], EquiBot [19], EquAct [7] and SAVLA [8] build policies whose output trans-forms consistently when the scene is rotated or translated, and general-purpose architectures such as the geometric-algebra transformer (GATr) [17] provide the underlying machinery. These methods obtain a strong geometric representation of the objects, but they represent the robot only through its end-effector pose. SAVLA is the closest to our layer in con-struction, carrying typed scalar and vector channels through its action head, yet it has no per-link tokens, no relative transforms between them and no joint motion subspaces. Our layer differs from these architectures in its aim and in its symmetry. Their defining property is equivariance to one transformation of the whole scene, with every feature expressed in one common frame. In Screw Attention, invari-ance is a consequence of building the layer on the screw algebra of rigid-body mechanics rather than its aim, and the same construction reproduces classical computations of robot mechanics. Its symmetry group is also larger: the global frame change handled by existing equivariant architectures is the special case in which every token’s frame is transformed in the same way (Appendix C). Finally, the first two strands have been geometric about different halves of the problem, the robot’s structure and the scene’s geometry. Our layer applies the same typing to the robot’s own bodies and to the objects of the scene, within a single token space. 

Kinematics-structured policies. A third strand brings the robot’s kinematics inside the network rather than encoding only its connectivity. XMoP [9] tokenises the links as poses and predicts link-wise pose transformations to transfer across manipulators. The Rodrigues Network [10] makes forward kinematics a learnable operator as a prior for action learning, and ABD-NET [20] mirrors the articulated-body algorithm in a graph network with learnable inertia parameters. These methods carry the same quantities that our layer uses, but XMoP and the Rodrigues Network process them with learned matrices applied directly to geometric coordinates, and ABD-NET replaces the edges and the inertias by learned ones. These operations do not in general commute with a change of frame. The output can then depend on how the reference frames are attached to the links, a modelling convention rather than a physical property. XMoP compensates for this with frame-randomising data augmentation and reports losing 

68 .1% of its success rate on unseen embodiments without it. Our screw attention layer, instead, keeps the transforms, screws and inertias as the model’s own quantities and the geometry inside operations that commute with a change of frame. Messages are transported by the relative pose between bodies rather than mapped from their coordinates. This is also what makes the velocity recursion an exact special case of the layer (Section III-D) rather than a function it may approximate. 

Physics outside the architecture. A final strand introduces physical knowledge without constraining the network itself. The policy of [21] achieves equivariance to changes in the definitions of the end-effector and camera frames through an analytic decoder. This is possible because its inputs, images and language, do not change when these frames are redefined. In our setting, however, altering the per-link frames changes every quantity the policy reads, so the invariance must be a property of the network rather than the decoder. Other approaches keep physics in the objective or in a sep-arate model. PIPER [22] regularises the policy objective with analytic dynamics, and KING [23] represents the robot as a kinematic graph but estimates its motion instead of predicting commands. By contrast, residual policy learning [24], [25] keeps an analytic controller at run time and learns a correction added to its output with an unstructured network. These approaches constrain the objective, the prediction target or the command, and are actually compatible with the constraint on the function class proposed in this paper. III. M ETHOD

B. The attention layer 

Pairings. In standard transformers, the attention weights depend only on the token features. In our approach, we also want these attention weights to depend on the geometric relation between what two tokens carry, such as their current velocities, a joint axis or the goal. To make this relation frame-independent, we use the two frame-invariant bilinear forms on twists: the Killing form κ and the Klein form λ (the reciprocal product of screw theory) [26], [27], which we call pairings. For two twists m = [ ω; v] and m′ = [ ω′; v′] expressed in the same frame, these pairings are 

κ(m, m′) = ω · ω′, λ(m, m′) = ω · v′ + v · ω′. (2) The following two examples show what the pairings measure in specific cases. For a joint axis S = [ a; p × a] and a twist 

m,

κ(S, m) = a · ω, λ(S, m) = a · (v + ω × p). (3) These are the rate at which m rotates about the axis, and the rate at which m slides along the axis. For two rotations about axes a and a′ through points p and p′,

κ = a·a′ = cos θ, λ = ( p−p′)·(a×a′) = ± δ sin θ, (4) where θ is the angle between the axes and δ the distance between them. In fact, every frame-independent symmetric bilinear form on twists is a weighted sum of the pairings κ

and λ [26], so no other pairing is needed. Note that the dot product of a standard transformer is purposely not used here since, applied to twists, it is frame-dependent. Indeed, moving the origin of the frame by r changes the linear part of each twist from v to v + ω × r, and therefore changes m⊤m′. By contrast, the pairings κ and λ are preserved under any change of frame. Accordingly, in the layer, a twist mj of token j is first transformed into the frame of token i as Xij mj . Masks. The H attention heads of each layer are split into two groups. An outward head lets a robot token attend to itself and to the tokens of its ancestors in the kinematic tree. Additionally, every robot token attends to every scene token, in both groups, while a scene token attends only to itself. We write M(i) for the set of tokens that token i may attend to in a given head. Pairs outside M(i) receive no attention weight; the token i itself is in M(i) in every head.

The residual gate is optional. Without it, the learned term is added directly to the model term, as in residual policy learning. The content of the model term and the use of the gate are chosen per task (Section IV-C).

A. Tasks 

We validated our screw attention approach on three robot tasks in simulation and one published benchmark (Fig. 4) described as follows. 

Reorientation. This task consisted of reorienting a payload with a fixed-base arm in episodes of 2.0 s at 50 Hz. Goals were drawn from one hemisphere of SO (3) in training and the policy evaluated on both. The performance metric was rollout success defined as the angle error <0.1 rad held for 20 steps, over 512 fixed episodes. 

Affordance. This task consisted of reaching an object along one of the K = 8 candidate approach frames, distributed over a full sphere around the object. A candidate was considered feasible when the analytic controller, run from the arm’s current configuration, could reach it. In each scene only a subset of the candidates was feasible. The policy was never told which candidates were feasible, and had to select one by moving toward it without colliding with the object. The performance metrics were the fraction of episodes in which a feasible frame was reached and the fraction in which the arm hit the object, over 1024 fixed scenes. 

Insertion. This task consisted of inserting a peg welded to the flange of the arm into a socket fixed in space, which involved contact. This setting evaluated the torque residual as both the analytical and learned components were used for the command as specified in Section III-C. The performance metric was insertion success, reported as a margin over the analytic controller alone on the same episodes, and split according to whether the episode lay within the reach of the controller. 

LIBERO-Spatial. Since every comparison on the previ-ous tasks was made against an analytic controller, we also evaluated the approach on a published benchmark. We used the ten tasks of LIBERO-Spatial [30]. In this suite, a named object must be picked up and placed at a destination (within 3 cm of the destination centre). H. Joint offsets 

The relative poses and joint screws of the robot tokens are computed from the robot’s kinematic model and its joint angles. The hand pose used by the target relations is computed in the same way. On a real robot, this model is often only ap-proximately calibrated. To emulate calibration error, we added a constant offset to every arm joint angle in the observation, while the simulator retained the true angles. The offsets were drawn once per episode from a Gaussian distribution and held fixed for the whole episode. The analytic controller received the same corrupted state. Offsets with a standard deviation of 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 ◦ displace the hand by 5, 10, 20 and 41 mm on average over the demonstration states. For reference, the factory kinematic model of Franka arms places the hand 3.3 to 8.8 mm from its true position on average [34], corresponding to offsets between 0.25 and 0.5 ◦.Fig. 8b shows the drop from canonical success. Within the range of the factory calibration, Screw Attention loses 0.4 points at 0.25 ◦ and 1.7 points at 0.5 ◦, suggesting that the learned policy can tolerate the errors of an uncalibrated Franka arm. The analytic controller loses 0.6 and 6.0 points at the same offsets. At 1 ◦, Screw Attention loses 13.9 points and the analytic controller 20.8. At 2 ◦, however, both fall off sharply: Screw Attention loses 42.5 points and the analytic controller loses 49.0 points. V. D ISCUSSION 

Analysing the results across tasks identifies the configura-tions in which the geometric structure of Screw Attention is beneficial (and where it is not), and clarifies which property of the network produces the gains. 

A. Where the geometry contributes, and where it does not 

The geometry contributes most on the affordance task and least on the insertion task, although both tasks use the same number of tokens. The difference lies in what the rest of the system already provides. In the insertion task, the analytic controller is part of the network through the model term (Eq. 15), so the geometry required by the task is supplied correctly at every step. This likely explains why corrupting the policy’s own copy of that geometry has little effect. In the affordance task, by contrast, nothing in the loop encodes which candidate is reachable. The policy must infer reachability from the relation between the arm and each candidate, and corrupt-ing that relation costs 73.9 points. The geometric structure pays when it carries information essential to the task that no other part of the system provides. Appendix H shows three task designs that fall into the same category as the insertion task: these reduce to estimating one object pose and moving to it, which the scalar features already allow. 

B. What the equivariance constraint provides, and what it does not 

The equivariance constraint provides robustness to how the robot is described. Under a frame change, the constrained networks retain nearly all of their success, whereas the uncon-strained network loses about half on average and the LIBERO controls lose nearly all. Although the equivariance constraint does not reduce the number of demonstrations needed, it changes what the network learns. An unconstrained network can reach the same success by fitting the frame convention of its training data, a shortcut that fails when the convention changes. The attention mask is what reduces the number of demonstrations needed. Among the geometric perturbations on LIBERO-Spatial, the frame change is the one that separates the networks. Pose noise and object displacement reduce the success of all learned networks by similar amounts, and Screw Attention keeps the lead it has on the canonical episodes. In Screw Attention, this robustness follows from the construction and requires none of SCREW ATTENTION 12 

the augmentation over frame conventions that other methods need (Section II). A network that is equivariant only to a global frame change, such as GATr [17], does not have this robustness by construction. Indeed, redefining the frame of each link in-dependently changes the relative transforms between links. No single global frame change can reproduce these independent per-link redefinitions, so such local changes lie outside the symmetry handled by such architectures (Appendix C). 

C. Why the learned policies tolerate observation errors 

Under pose noise and joint-angle offsets, Screw Attention loses less success than the analytic controller that receives the same corrupted observations (Fig. 8). The analytic controller relies on precise, discrete decisions, such as closing the gripper only when the hand is within a few millimetres of its target. A small observation error can then trigger such a decision at the wrong moment or prevent it altogether. The learned policies, by contrast, map the observation continuously to commands. A small error shifts the command without altering the overall course of the task. Under pose noise, this tolerance is shared by all learned networks. It disappears once the error exceeds what a grasp can absorb. VI. L IMITATIONS 

As this study focused on the representation rather than on its deployment, Screw Attention was validated only in simu-lation. In this setting, the policies read object poses from the simulator. A deployment therefore requires a pose estimator. We emulated the pose estimation errors by adding translation noise to the object poses (Section IV-G), but rotation errors were not tested. Similarly, photometric factors, such as camera placement, lighting, background and texture, are outside the scope of this paper, since they reach the policy only through the pose estimator. Validating the approach on a real robot, with an estimator in the loop, is one of the main directions for future work. Additionally, because the rigid-body algebra is inside the network, Screw Attention depends on the accuracy of the robot model. The pair terms and inertias come from it, and the model errors were tested only as joint offsets (Section IV-H), not as errors in link lengths or inertias. Finally, our construction assumes that each body is driven by one revolute or prismatic joint, which holds for the fixed-base serial arms studied here but excludes bodies with several joints and floating bases (mobile manipulators, legged robots). Screw Attention is also the slowest of the compared networks at inference, since it transforms the geometric channels of every ordered pair of tokens, a cost that grows quadratically with the number of tokens. It nevertheless remains far below the control period at the 22 tokens used here (Appendix A). VII. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced Screw Attention, an architecture that in-tegrates rigid-body algebra inside a transformer layer. With robot bodies and scene objects as input tokens, the relative pose and the joint screw of each pair of bodies transform the messages exchanged between tokens, while the attention weights see only frame-invariant quantities. Instead of learning the geometry from data, the network is constrained to respect it by construction. This is achieved by integrating geometric quantities provided by the robot model. The learned weights mix only their channels, never their spatial components. The robot’s analytic model thus becomes an input to the network. When an analytic controller is available, the network can also learn a residual on its command, weighted by a learned gate. With this construction, a single transformer layer can express the velocity recursion along the kinematic chain, and the transported messages do not depend on how frames are attached to links. On four tasks, Screw Attention matched or outperformed other learned networks of the same size and exceeded the analytic controller on three of them. On LIBERO-Spatial, a policy with 16,162 parameters reaches 97.3%, above networks of the same size and above a flat network with 27 times more parameters. We showed that the geometric structure is most beneficial when the task depends on relations between frames that no other part of the system supplies. On an affordance task, correct relations are worth 73.9 points over shuffled ones. On an insertion task, where the analytic controller already supplies the geometry, the margin is small, but the learned residual adds 17.3 points to that controller. The equivariance constraint makes the policy robust to how the robot is described, and the attention mask reduces the number of demonstrations needed. On the reorientation task, a frame change costs Screw Attention 0.6 points while a policy without this constraint loses 51.7 points. Moreover, Screw Attention tolerates pose noise up to 10 mm and joint offsets within the factory calibration of a Franka arm. Future work will validate the approach on a real robot with a pose estimator in the loop, and extend the construction to bodies driven by several joints and to moving bases, which would open it to legged robots, musculoskeletal models and other articulated rigid-body systems. GATr [17] builds E(3) -equivariant attention by representing every quantity as a multivector of the projective geometric al-gebra and combining multivectors with the geometric product. Screw Attention stands in a two-way relation to it, and the two directions point opposite ways. 

Algebra. Our geometric channels are the bivectors of that algebra, which form the Lie algebra se (3) of screws under the commutator. A twist is a bivector, a grade-2 element of the algebra. A spatial transform Xij is the adjoint action of a motor, the element of the algebra that represents a rigid displacement, on screws. Finally, the pairings κ and λ of Eq. 2 are the frame-invariant bilinear forms, which map two screws to a number and are unchanged when both are moved to another frame. We keep only the grade that rigid-body mechanics uses, and Section III-D shows that this restriction suffices for the two computations considered there: a single layer reproduces the velocity recursion and the Jacobian-transpose law. 

Symmetry. Here the containment is reversed. GATr is equivariant to a change of the one common frame of the whole scene, whereas each of our tokens carries its own frame, changed independently by the transforms Ti of Eq. 16. In the common-frame representation of Appendix B, GATr’s symme-try is the diagonal subgroup of our per-token gauge, in which every Ti is the same transform (without reflections, which 

E(3) includes). A model equivariant only to this subgroup is not invariant to re-describing each link independently, since that changes the relative transforms Xij in a way no change of the common frame can reproduce. Such a model must learn the invariance from data. Ours is invariant by construction, up to the three frame-dependent pair features of Appendix D. This holds only for the torque readouts. The twist readout is invariant to rotations only (Section III-E).

On a fixed-base manipulator, we expect the scene relation to add little in two kinds of task: reaching a goal defined by the position of one object, and avoiding obstacles with a policy that reacts to the nearest one. Because the base frame does not move, the policy needs only one vector per object, or per link, SCREW ATTENTION 17 

expressed in the base frame, and the scalar features already supply this information. The contribution of the relation should be more prominent when the objective is itself a relation between frames, as in the affordance task, where the policy must choose which of several candidate frames the arm can reach. Two tasks support this hypothesis. 

Reaching through clutter. A policy given the full scene was compared with one given only the nearest obstacle to each link. With a reactive demonstrator, the difference was 2.3 ±

1.5 points, within the variation between seeds, as expected if the nearest obstacle to each link carries the information that avoidance needs. 

Reaching a single pose. On the task score (success minus five times the collision rate), Screw Attention outperformed scalar only by 0.318 and shuffled transforms by 0.319, over four seeds. The margin came mainly from representing the goal orientation as a screw on the end-effector token, rather than from the scene relation. The object’s geometric channels were zero, so permuting the object’s transforms left the output of the layer unchanged. Whether a task depends on relations between frames can be checked before training, by regressing the demonstrator’s action on object positions alone and on positions and orien-tations, and by testing whether more than one relation affects the action.
